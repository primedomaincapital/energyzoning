What a Solar Permit Costs — and Why the Same Project Pays Twice as Much in the Next County

The same six kilowatts cost $350 to permit in Phoenix and $1,695 in San Francisco. At utility scale the spread is wider — and the fee is the smallest part of what the county actually charges.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The panels are the same. The inverter is the same. The crew drives the same truck. The only thing that changes is the county line — and on a six-kilowatt rooftop system, that line is worth $1,345. In Phoenix, the permit costs $350 all-in: a $250 flat fee through SolarAPP+, no plan review charge, and a $100 interconnection fee. In San Francisco, the same system costs $1,695: $850 for the building permit, $400 to the fire department, $300 for plan review, and $145 to the utility. The hardware does not know the difference. The paperwork does, and the paperwork is priced by the jurisdiction that issues it.

The national numbers make the spread look modest until you do the division. The National Renewable Energy Laboratory’s 2023 benchmarking study found that permitting, inspection, and interconnection average 7.5 percent of total installed residential solar cost — about $0.42 per watt on a $5.60-per-watt median 2026 price. Across the 500 jurisdictions NREL analyzed, permit costs ran from $150 to $2,200 for the same residential system, a spread of nearly fifteen to one. The city-by-city figures for a typical six-kilowatt system fill in the middle: Austin, $631; Denver, $768; Boston, $730; Miami-Dade, $845; Los Angeles, $1,061. The average American home solar project runs about $31,000, and roughly 30 percent of it — about $9,000 — is what the industry calls soft costs: permit fees, inspection scheduling, forms, and the hours an installer spends working through each town’s own process instead of putting hardware on a roof. That figure comes from Heaven Campbell, the Florida program director at the nonprofit Solar United Neighbors, and it is the number that explains why two identical quotes can differ by thousands of dollars before a single panel is ordered.

At utility scale the same mechanisms apply to a much larger base, and the fee schedule is only the first of several bills. A utility-scale project does not pay a flat permit fee; it pays fees tied to project valuation, and then it pays for the studies the county requires before it will issue the permit at all — environmental review, traffic analysis, glare studies, drainage plans, and the engineering to prove the array will not flood the neighbor’s field. The permit package alone, before any hardware is bought, typically runs $1,000 to $3,200 in soft costs for the paperwork on a residential job; at utility scale the equivalent package is measured in months of consultant time rather than dollars on a fee schedule. The deeper cost is the one that never appears on an invoice: the review itself. A jurisdiction that takes thirty days to approve a permit and a jurisdiction that takes six months are charging different prices for the identical project, because the developer carries the land, the interconnection deposit, and the financing through every week of the wait. Florida understood this before most states did. House Bill 683, signed June 13, 2025 and effective that July, gives local governments five business days to review and approve a single-trade permit like rooftop solar, lets licensed private providers do the plan review and inspections, allows those inspections to happen virtually, and bars building departments from demanding a copy of the installer’s contract before issuing a permit. BloombergNEF projects Florida reaches 710 megawatts of home solar in 2026, up 62 percent from 439 megawatts in 2025 — and in the year’s first thirteen weeks, the state led the country in approved solar permits with more than 450 megawatts’ worth, roughly four times the same stretch in either of the two prior years.

The permit fee is only the first of the jurisdiction’s charges, and the inspection is the one that catches families by surprise. After the permit is issued, the system still has to be inspected before it can be energized, and the inspection has its own queue: the national average from submission to approval runs about twenty-eight days, and the permit package alone typically carries $1,000 to $3,200 in soft costs for the paperwork before any hardware is bought. Some jurisdictions charge a flat fee regardless of system size. Others charge by valuation, by watt, or by the number of inspections required — and the fee structure itself creates part of the spread, because a valuation-based fee on a $31,000 system is a different number than a flat fee on the same system. The jurisdictions that have adopted SolarAPP+ have collapsed both the fee and the wait into a single instant issue, which is why Phoenix’s $350 total is the floor of the national table and not an outlier. The jurisdictions that have not adopted it are not necessarily charging more for the permit. They are charging for the process — and the process is priced in weeks.

The programs built to shrink the spread are the most useful evidence that it can be shrunk. SolarAPP+, the Department of Energy’s automated permitting platform, issues a standard residential permit instantly in participating jurisdictions — Phoenix’s $250 flat fee is a SolarAPP+ number — and the SunShot program’s SolSmart Gold standard, together with SolarAPP+, targets getting permitting and interconnection below $0.20 per watt, less than half the current national average. The jurisdictions that have adopted them are the ones at the bottom of the cost table. The ones that have not are the ones at the top, and the reason is rarely the fee itself. A $200 permit fee in a town that takes eight weeks to issue it costs more than a $500 fee in a town that takes five days, because the developer’s carrying costs run every week the project sits in review. The fee schedule is the visible price. The calendar is the real one.

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves more respect than the solar industry usually gives it. Cheap permits are not free, and fast permits are not automatically good ones. SolarAPP+ works because the design it approves is standardized — the same panels, the same racking, the same electrical layout, checked against a fixed set of rules. A utility-scale project with custom engineering, a floodplain, and a neighbor who hires a lawyer cannot be rubber-stamped, and the review that costs money is also the review that catches the fire risk, the drainage failure, and the structural shortcut. San Francisco’s $400 fire department fee buys a fire review that Phoenix’s instant permit does not, and the historic-overlay review that pushes its total to $2,200 is the same review that keeps a crew from bolting panels onto a roof that cannot hold them. The counties that charge the most are not always the ones doing the least. Sometimes they are the ones doing the most, and the developer who complains about the fee is complaining about the only part of the process that protects the project from itself.

The implication for anyone pricing a solar project is to price the jurisdiction before pricing the panels. Get the fee schedule, the plan-review timeline, and the inspection backlog in writing from the authority having jurisdiction — and get the same three numbers from the county next door, because the project that is economic in one and uneconomic in the other is the same project. Ask the installer how many days permitting actually takes in that specific city or county, and get the answer in writing: a five-day process and a two-month process are two different prices for the identical system, and that difference reaches the quote whether anyone itemizes it or not. The panels will cost the same wherever they are installed. The county is a line item, not an afterthought — and on the current national numbers, it is worth about a third of the project.
