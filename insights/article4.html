Two Thousand Gigawatts Are Waiting on Paperwork

The interconnection queue holds 1.5 times the entire installed U.S. grid. The median project waits five years. Thirteen percent ever get built — and the gas rush just changed what the queue is for.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 9, 2026 · 6 min read

Somewhere in the queue is 2,061 gigawatts of generation and storage capacity — about 8,200 projects, actively seeking permission to connect to the American grid at the end of 2025, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s Queued Up 2026 Edition. The country’s entire installed generating fleet is 1,374 gigawatts. The queue is one and a half power grids, waiting on paperwork. The median project that actually reached commercial operation in 2025 spent 61 months in the queue, up from 22 months in 2008. Of all the capacity that entered the queue between 2000 and 2020, only 13% was ever built. The rest — 75% — was withdrawn before it generated a single watt. For every gigawatt that reached commercial operation in 2025, approximately 14 gigawatts were withdrawn.

The mix inside the queue tells the story of the last two years. Solar stands at 773 gigawatts, down 19% year over year. Storage, 99% batteries, stands at 749 gigawatts, down 16%. The single most consequential data point is the one that went the other way: natural gas surged to 253 gigawatts. The queue is not a list of what developers dream of building. It is a list of what the price signals reward, and the price signals have started rewarding gas again — for data centers that need power on a schedule, and for a grid that needs capacity it can dispatch.

The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission tried to fix the queue in July 2023 with Order 2023, replacing first-come, first-served serial studies with first-ready, first-served cluster studies, with penalties for speculative entries and shorter study timelines. Three years on, the order has done little to clear the backlog. Berkeley Lab notes that most of the data in its 2026 edition predate the reforms and that it is too early to measure their effect — which is the polite way of saying the queue is still 2,061 gigawatts deep. The reform changed how projects are studied. It did not change how many transmission lines get built.

Texas is the control group. ERCOT, which is not subject to FERC regulation, had about 8.6 gigawatts of operational storage at the beginning of 2025. By early 2026 that number had spiked to around 14 gigawatts, an increase of over 60%, enough to serve about 15 to 16 percent of ERCOT’s 85 to 91 gigawatts of peak demand. PJM, the largest wholesale market in the country, has less than one gigawatt of operational storage against a peak system load above 150 gigawatts. In ERCOT, projects move through interconnection studies in as little as one year and reach commercial operation in as little as three. In the FERC-regulated markets, the median is five years and climbing. The difference is not the sun or the wind. It is the rules.

The honest version is that a shrinking queue is not entirely bad news. Solar fell 19% and storage 16% partly because speculative projects withdrew — the filter working, not the system failing. A queue that peaked at roughly 2,290 gigawatts at the end of 2024 and fell to 2,061 gigawatts a year later is a queue shedding projects that were never going to be built. And the load is finding its own way around the queue: a February 2026 analysis identified 46 data center projects, with a combined capacity of 56 gigawatts, planning to build their own generation behind the meter. Hyperscaler capital spending is approaching $700 billion in aggregate 2026 guidance across Microsoft, Meta, Amazon, and Alphabet. When the queue takes five years, the largest buyers of power stop queuing.

That is the implication for anyone whose asset is a piece of land with a queue position. The queue is the map, and the map now says two things at once: permitted, studied, ready-to-build projects are the scarcest asset in American energy, and the buyers with the most money are learning to live without the grid. A project with an interconnection agreement, a county permit, and a site that can host storage is worth more than the megawatts on its nameplate. The zoning hearing, the setback ordinance, the county vote — those are the gates now. The queue did not make energy about engineering. It made it about permission.
