The Battery Siting Fight: Why Storage Projects Face a Different Zoning Battle

California’s battery fleet grew from under 700 megawatts to more than 21,000 in seven years — and almost every megawatt of it was fought over at a county hearing first.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

In seven years, California built the largest battery fleet on earth — from under 700 megawatts in 2019 to 21,112 megawatts by August 2026 — and almost none of it arrived quietly. Every large project passed through a county hearing where the same three questions were asked in the same order: will it catch fire, how close is it to my house, and who is responsible when it does. The questions are fair. The answers, over five years of fires, moratoria, and new state laws, have rearranged who gets to answer them.

The fire record is short and specific, which is why it carries so much weight. The Moss Landing facility, then the world’s largest battery installation, suffered overheating incidents in September 2021 and February 2022, both traced to malfunctions in its water-based heat suppression system. In September 2023, a fire at Terra-Gen’s 140-megawatt Valley Center facility in San Diego County’s backcountry put battery fire risk on the radar of rural communities. The Gateway fire in Otay Mesa in May 2024 burned with periodic flare-ups for roughly two weeks. The Escondido fire that September forced evacuations of nearby schools and businesses. Each incident was contained without injuries, and each one changed the politics of the next project more than the engineering of the last one. The local response came in two forms that would define the next two years. Solano County enacted a two-year pause on front-of-the-meter battery storage in January 2024 — California’s first county-level moratorium — explicitly to buy time to write safety-focused land-use standards. San Diego County took the other path, writing standards without a moratorium. Both counties ended up in the same place: a set of rules that treats a battery as a distinct land use, with its own setbacks, its own fire plan, and its own emergency response requirements.

The rules that emerged are now the template other states copy. NFPA 855, the national standard for stationary energy storage, and the 2024 International Fire Code require listed equipment — batteries to UL 1973, systems to UL 9540 — plus fire and explosion testing, a hazard mitigation analysis, an emergency response plan, and responder training. The zoning side of it is measured in feet. Tucson’s proposed standards, published in October 2026, set a 150-foot residential separation for large outdoor systems — at the top of the 50-to-150-foot range the American Planning Association reports nationally, and at the lower end of the Arizona jurisdictions Tucson reviewed — and 300 feet from schools, daycares, hospitals, senior living, and assembly uses. Illinois wrote the same logic into statute: since June 1, 2026, counties may not ban storage systems above 1,000 kilowatts in agricultural or industrial zones, but they may require NFPA 855 and UL 1973 and UL 9540 listing. Washington is the only state that lets a developer choose a state or local permitting pathway regardless of project size. Everywhere else, the pathway depends on where the project sits and how big it is — and that uncertainty, more than any single rule, is what the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory’s March 2026 report for the Department of Energy identifies as the core siting problem.

The patchwork is the problem, and the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory’s March 2026 report for the Department of Energy says so directly: the core siting difficulty is not any single rule but the uncertainty about which pathway a project must follow, because the pathway depends on where the project sits and how big it is. Washington is the only state that lets a developer choose a state or local permitting pathway regardless of project size. Illinois bars counties from banning storage above 1,000 kilowatts in agricultural or industrial zones but lets them require NFPA 855 and UL listing. Solano County banned nothing permanently but paused everything for two years while it wrote standards. San Diego County wrote standards without pausing at all. A developer with the same battery in four counties faces four different processes, four different setbacks, and four different emergency-plan requirements — and the cost of learning each one is paid in months. The industry’s answer to the patchwork is the state opt-in: California’s AB 205, which trades local control for a single consolidated permit from the California Energy Commission. The counties’ answer is that the opt-in exists because they wrote the rules that made it necessary, and that a developer who wants local approval should bring a local fire plan instead of a state preemption.

California’s answer to the uncertainty was to take the decision away from the counties. Assembly Bill 205, passed in June 2022, created an opt-in certification program that lets clean energy projects — battery storage of 200 megawatt-hours or more — seek a consolidated permit directly from the California Energy Commission instead of any local permit or ordinance, provided specific safety measures are demonstrated. The result is the self-reinforcing cycle the last five years produced: a high-profile incident triggers a local moratorium or ban; the moratorium pushes developers toward the AB 205 pathway, which strips local authority but enforces higher standards; developers invoking AB 205 for unrelated requirements provoke further local distrust, plus a few more incidents; and the response is more sophisticated local regulation and higher statewide safety standards that gradually raise the floor for everyone. The net result is not a slowdown in deployment — the fleet grew thirty-fold while the fights were happening — but increasing pressure toward industry safety improvements driven by local concern. The counties that fought hardest did not stop the batteries. They made them safer.

Here is the counterargument, and it cuts both ways. The fire record is real but small relative to the fleet: a few dozen incidents across tens of thousands of megawatts, none with injuries, against a technology that now stores a meaningful share of California’s evening power. The industry’s point is that the same incidents drove the standards — NFPA 855, UL 9540A fire testing, the hazard mitigation analysis — that make new projects safer than the ones that burned, and that a county judging a 2026 project by a 2021 fire is judging the wrong machine. The opposition’s point is that a standard is only as good as its enforcement at two in the morning in a rural county, and that the difference between a listed battery and a safe installation is the fire department that knows how to fight it. Both points are true, which is why the fight has moved from whether storage gets built to how far it sits from a school and whose emergency plan is on file. The setback is the compromise both sides can live with: far enough that a fire is a property problem rather than a life-safety problem, close enough that the project still pencils.

The implication for developers is to bring the fire plan to the first hearing, not the third. The projects that win fastest are the ones that arrive with NFPA 855 compliance, UL-listed equipment, a hazard mitigation analysis, and a written emergency response plan already agreed with the local fire department — because the county’s real question is not whether the battery is safe in the abstract, but whether anyone will know what to do when the alarm sounds. The implication for counties is the mirror image: a moratorium buys time to write standards, but the standards are the point, and a county that writes them well keeps both the tax base and the authority. California’s fleet will keep growing either way. The only question the zoning fight still decides is who writes the rules for the next 21,000 megawatts — the county, or the state agency the county forced the developer to go to.
