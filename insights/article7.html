The Interconnection Clock: What Happens After the Zoning Approval Arrives

The zoning hearing took eighteen months. The grid study will take five years. The project that wins the county and loses the queue is the most expensive failure in American energy.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The developer had already won the hard part. Eighteen months of county hearings, two environmental reviews, and a zoning approval with conditions the neighbors could live with. Then the utility sent the interconnection study, and the project that had taken eighteen months to permit was told it would take five years to connect. The median project that reached commercial operation in 2025 spent 61 months — just over five years — between its interconnection request and operation, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s Queued Up: 2026 Edition. In 2008 the same wait was 22 months. In 2015 it was 36. The queue has tripled in length while the projects in it have not gotten any simpler, and the zoning approval that once felt like the finish line now reads like the starting gun for a second, longer race.

The scale of the queue is what makes the wait hard to grasp. At the end of 2025, about 8,200 projects were actively seeking interconnection across the United States, representing 2,061 gigawatts — 1,312 gigawatts of generation and 749 gigawatts of storage. That is one and a half times the entire installed American power plant fleet, which Berkeley Lab puts at 1,374 gigawatts. Most of it will never run: of the capacity that asked to connect between 2000 and 2020, only 13 percent had reached commercial operation by the end of 2025, while 75 percent had been withdrawn and 10 percent was still waiting. In 2025, about fourteen gigawatts were withdrawn for every gigawatt that was built. The queue also changed shape while it grew: active natural gas capacity rose 86 percent in 2025 to 253 gigawatts, while solar, wind, and storage each fell around 16 to 19 percent. The gas rush is the market’s answer to a queue that rewards whatever can be built fastest — and the 549 gigawatts that already hold a draft or executed interconnection agreement but have not yet been built are the projects that cleared the queue and are still waiting on something else.

The clock has two hands, and only one of them is the study. From the interconnection request to commercial operation, the median project built in 2025 took 61 months. From a signed interconnection agreement to operation, it took 31 months — up from 21 months in 2008. The difference between those two numbers is the study period itself: roughly thirty months of engineering review before the agreement is even signed, and thirty-one months of construction and upgrade work after it. The studies are sequential and interdependent, because what one project requires depends on which other projects proceed, and the cost allocation is only known at the end. A developer can spend years in the process and then discover that the required network upgrades make the project uneconomic. The expensive part is usually not the plant’s own connection but the network upgrades — new lines, transformers, and substation equipment elsewhere on the grid that the new project would overload. When ISO New England finished its transitional cluster study in August 2026, it put an estimated $65.3 million on a 125-megawatt battery in Tewksbury, Massachusetts, $56.3 million of it network upgrades; the developer withdrew the project. The average across the study’s 23 projects was $178 million. Berkeley Lab’s own summary is blunter: limited transmission capacity has produced high upgrade costs that contribute to high withdrawals. And because withdrawal by one project changes the cost allocation for the others, withdrawals cascade, forcing restudies that lengthen the queue for everyone remaining.

The withdrawals do not just shrink the queue. They change it, because the cost of a network upgrade is allocated among the projects in a cluster, and when one project withdraws, the allocation for the projects that remain has to be recalculated. The recalculation is called a restudy, and it is the queue’s cruelest mechanism: a project that has already waited thirty months can be sent back for another study because a different project left, and the new study can produce a new upgrade bill that makes the remaining project uneconomic, which triggers another withdrawal and another restudy. Berkeley Lab’s figures show the scale of the churn — of the capacity that asked to connect between 2000 and 2020, 75 percent withdrew, 13 percent was built, and 10 percent was still waiting at the end of 2025, a quarter century after some of those requests were filed. The projects still in the queue are not the speculative ones. They are the ones that survived the filter and are still waiting on the grid to catch up with them. FERC Order 2023 tried to break the cycle with readiness requirements — financial deposits and site control that make a speculative request expensive — but the deposits filter the applicants, not the grid. The grid is still the constraint, and no readiness requirement builds a transmission line.

The reform that was supposed to fix this is FERC Order No. 2023, which replaced the old first-come, first-served serial studies with cluster studies — groups of projects studied together, with readiness requirements and financial commitments meant to weed out the speculative. It has helped at the margins and not at the core, because the core problem is not the order of the studies but the capacity of the grid they study. Some states have started pricing the problem differently. Massachusetts, where a homeowner whose system happened to overload a neighborhood transformer once paid for the whole replacement, approved a replacement in August 2026 that takes effect November 1: a flat $225 fee paid by every applicant with a listed inverter-based solar or battery system of 25 kilowatts or less, with the pooled money covering up to $10,000 of upgrades for Eversource and National Grid customers — $1,450 for Unitil — and anything above that still falling to the applicant. It is a small fix for small systems. There is no equivalent fix for a 125-megawatt battery facing a $56.3 million network upgrade bill.

Here is the counterargument, and it deserves a fair hearing before anyone calls the queue broken. The queue is not a line. It is a filter, and most of the 2,061 gigawatts in it was never going to be built. Developers file interconnection requests speculatively — the same project in three queues, the same site with three sizes — because a request is cheap and an option is valuable. The 13 percent that gets built is the point, not the failure: the queue’s job is to sort the serious from the speculative, and the 75 percent that withdraws is the sorting working as designed. The counter to that is the price of the sorting. The filter charges its fee in years, and years kill projects that would have been built. A developer who spends thirty months in studies and then withdraws on a $178 million upgrade bill has not been filtered. It has been bankrupted slowly, and the next developer with the same site starts the same thirty months over. A filter that takes five years to say no is not a filter. It is a second permitting process with no appeal.

The implication for anyone with a permitted project is to price the interconnection study before the land. The zoning approval is the visible victory and the cheaper one; the grid study is where the project actually lives or dies, and its bill arrives last, when the developer has the least leverage and the most sunk cost. Ask the utility for the cluster study timeline and the network upgrade history for the substation before signing the land option, not after. Assume the median — sixty-one months from request to operation — and underwrite the carrying costs for all of it, because the project that budgets for three years and gets five does not finish late. It finishes insolvent. The queue will keep growing until transmission does, and transmission is the slowest thing in American energy to build. The developers who survive it are the ones who treat the interconnection clock as the project schedule, and the zoning clock as the warm-up.
